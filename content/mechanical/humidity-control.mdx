#### Whole-house dehumidification

Buildings with low cooling loads can be difficult to dehumidify with efficient heat-pump cooling systems. Cooling
systems tend to satisfy the thermostat setting quickly, before the system has had a chance to extract moisture from the
air. The cooling system then ramps down or turns off completely. In either case, dehumidification may be insufficient,
leading to uncomfortably high humidity levels in the home.

For this reason, and because there are times when no cooling is required but dehumidification may be desired, we
recommend at least planning for, if not installing, a dedicated whole-house dehumidification system. These systems can be
stand-alone or integrated with the heat-pump distribution system. In any case, high-efficiency systems, like those from
[Santa Fe](https://www.santa-fe-products.com/), should be selected.

#### Whole-house humidification

Excellent building airtightness and mechanical ventilation with heat and moisture recovery will help maintain healthy
indoor relative humidity levels. However, if the home may be intermittently occupied, whole-house humidification should
be considered, as wintertime humidity levels could fall below levels that are healthy and comfortable for occupants and
potentially damaging to finishes and furnishings. When selecting humidification systems, total energy use should be part
of the decision.

Bypass and fan-powered humidifiers add moisture by passing air through a moistened filter. They use relatively little
electricity, as moisture transfer happens more or less passively. Steam humidifiers, though more effective, use
considerable electricity to convert liquid water to steam. For more information, see [ENERGY STAR's scoping
report](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/asset/document/ENERGY_STAR_Scoping_Report_Residential_Humidifiers.pdf)
on residential humidifiers.
